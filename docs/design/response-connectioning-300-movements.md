# Response to "The 300+ Movements to a Fair Economy" (Connectioning, Jessica Friday)

Draft for posting. Companion to the outreach skill; proof states below are the point.

## The map needs a road under it

I read Jessica Friday's list twice. It ends on the sharpest line in it:

> "The problem was never the ideas. It was that nobody could see they were connected."

That is half the problem, and the half she is equipped to fix. She is building the visibility layer, and it is real work — 300 movements documented, a directory, an evaluation framework on the way, aliases cross-checked against Guy Dauncey's 146 names and the Dictionary of Radical Alternatives. Nobody has gathered them at that scale before.

But seeing each other is not the same as being able to act together. That is a second Tower of Babel problem, and nobody has built the layer it lives in.

Her list names the first one. Same ideas, different words, no shared name, no central HQ, no shared budget, so most of these movements don't know each other exist.

The second one is worse, because it survives contact. Two movements can find each other, agree completely, and still not be able to do anything together. They can't hold money jointly. They can't share a member list. They can't run a project on the same surface. They can't give each other access to anything and take it back later. Every group's tools are rented separately, and none of them is owned by anyone in the room.

That is why her list has cooperatives at the top and, three hundred entries later, still nothing that lets two groups *operate* as one thing.

### To form a tie, there has to be something to tie

She cites network weaving: birds of a feather flock together; those close by form a tie. Both are true. Both hide a requirement.

Two groups three blocks apart can meet today. They still can't merge their books. The tie they can form is a phone call and a shared spreadsheet. The infrastructure underneath them — where the money lands, where the member list lives, who holds the admin key — is not theirs, and is not shared.

Ask what a movement's shared asset actually needs, and it comes out as four properties. It has to be **group-controlled** (the group decides, not one person). **Permissioned** (everyone holds their role; no one holds all of it). **Durable** (it survives whoever set it up and whoever pays for it). **Owned** (by the collective, not by a card and a key).

Practice is the exact inverse. The website, the socials, the donor tool, the event pages almost always run on infrastructure that one organizer both pays for from a personal debit card and administers with the admin key. That person goes quiet, or their card lapses, and the movement goes dark by nobody's decision.

That isn't a cash-flow problem. It is the reason cooperation doesn't compound.

### What irl.coop is

irl.coop is the cooperative form applied to the infrastructure itself.

A group is the account. Not a login with a shared password — a Safe that the group controls, with seats, roles and votes, where access is scoped per member and revocable. Members get one identity across every tool instead of a new account per app. The tools sit on infrastructure the group runs: chat, documents and files, projects, events and public ticketing, mail on the group's own domain, maps, and search across the group's own knowledge. Money and records are meant to stay with the group, not with a platform.

Cost works differently, and it is the honest difference: it doesn't grow every time you add a member.

Federation is the part that matters here. Groups keep their own identity, their own rules, their own money. Nothing has to be agreed beyond the shared ground underneath. Jessica recommends Alignment Beyond Agreement — diversity in thinking is a feature, not a problem to resolve. That is exactly the design constraint. No central HQ is not a limitation we worked around; it is the requirement. irl.coop is a steward, not a root. Interconnect, not a directory.

### What is true right now

This audience is the one best able to catch a manufactured pitch, so here is the receipt instead of a claim.

irl.coop is one node, running, used in development. Identity, groups and roles, scoped access, documents, projects, events, mail, maps and knowledge search are built and working. The shielded treasury and the interconnection between separate groups are designed, not built. Nothing has yet been demonstrated inside a real group's daily work.

We keep that published. Every user-visible claim on our site is classified in a claims registry as shipped, designed or aspirational, and a maturity table records the gaps — including that no subsystem has reached real-group use yet. Jessica is right that the field has enough theory and needs building. The honest version of "we build" is a document that says what isn't finished.

### Why this is the right answer, and what we are not

Good tools already exist for parts of this: Open Collective puts money in the open, Loomio handles consent-based decisions, platform co-ops show worker ownership works on software. Each solves one layer, and most run on infrastructure the group doesn't hold.

None of them make the network possible. A tool you rent lets you act. Shared ground lets you act *together*, which is the thing the 300 movements can't do yet.

So this is not a competing project. The list is the map. This is a road under it. If a movement on that list keeps hitting the same wall — we found each other, and we still can't do this together — that wall is the problem worth building against.
