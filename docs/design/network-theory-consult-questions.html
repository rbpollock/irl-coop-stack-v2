Questions for a Network-Theory Consultation

Group health, extraction, and fair outcomes in a cooperative network · one hour

What I'm really trying to learn: how to detect unhealthy or extractive relationships early — and act on them — so that fair outcomes become the likely default, without the detection itself becoming a new form of control.

The model. Nodes are self-custodied accounts; groups are also nodes, and a node can own or join other nodes. Edges are membership, ownership, and value flow. I want to build early-warning signals — statistical and observational — that flag unhealthy or extractive relationships before they do harm.

  1. Detection, and the fairness baseline 15 min

    What are the reliable structural and behavioral signatures of an extractive or unhealthy relationship — the node that takes a toll on every path, the sink that receives without giving, long dependency chains, the asymmetry of who always asks versus who always gets asked? And against what should I actually measure fairness: I currently score reciprocity, flow, distribution, and persistence — is reciprocity the right primitive, and what makes a signal robust to the people who would game it?

  2. Stewardship vs. extraction 12 min

    When the structure looks identical — a parent group, a coordinator, a funder — what separates stewardship from extraction? Reciprocity over time, consent, the ability to exit, or something structural? And can the same shape be healthy in one context and extractive in another?

  3. Automating a flag — and the coop's own role in group health 12 min

    I want early warnings automated, but a flag can course-correct a group or become a weapon and a panopticon. How do I design it so it raises the chance of a fair outcome — who sees it, private nudge versus public rating, and where does the flag itself start causing the harm it detects? And beyond automation: what role can the cooperative itself play in supporting group health — a person who steps in? Would people volunteer for that, or does it need a stipend, and how does paying (or not paying) the helper shape whether their help is trusted?

  4. Decision-maker overload → opportunistic governance on real money 12 min

    Members sit as decision-makers in several groups at once. What can a person genuinely handle — how many groups, how much fiduciary weight — before judgment degrades? And how does that overload become a vector for harmful or opportunistic governance proposals that move real money, the kind that would never pass a less-stretched group?

  5. Intergroup pathologies, and cycles 8 min

    What new extraction appears between groups — a federation that is really a landlord, a vertical fund draining its locals, a parent hoarding? And are circular structures (A owns B owns A) a known way to hide extraction or make authority unaccountable?

  6. The economic model — free use, a fair-share badge, non-extractiveness by code 12 min

    6a · the fair-share signal. I plan free usage with transparent cost — every group sees exactly what it costs to host, and groups that cover their usage (or have it sponsored) earn a badge, on the bet that people prefer to partner and trade with groups paying their fair share. Is that signal strong enough to sustain the platform, and what unfairness does it create — a two-tier system where the badge marks the rich, or punishes groups who genuinely cannot pay?

    6b · bounding against drift. The long-term funding is small fees on commerce, bookings, and custody interest, percentages the members set. The risk is a majority someday voting the coop into extractive territory. Can “sustainable but not extractive” be enshrined in enforceable code — hard bounds a majority cannot amend — or is hard-coding values a brittle way to bound against drift?

One hour. Questions 1–4 are the spine; 5 and 6 can yield to time — and 6 splits cleanly into 6a (behavioral signal) and 6b (constitutional design) if the hour runs short.
