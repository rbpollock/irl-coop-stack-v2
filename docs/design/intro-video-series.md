# Intro video series — strategy

Status: proposed · 2026-09-13 · audience = prospective member groups + co-op organizers
Companion: `irl-coop-explainer-slides.html` (network-theory review deck), `group-model-technical-overview.html`,
landing page copy (`apps/web/irl-dashboard`), `event-bus-and-group-shapes.md` (the economy story).

## 0. The answer in one paragraph

Eight standalone explainer videos is a quarter of production before anyone can use the thing. Instead:
**one 12-minute master film** cut into **four short cuts**, produced in **reverse order of ambition** —
*proof first* (real screen recordings of work a real group actually did), *money second* (the cost
comparison — the only video that creates urgency), *education last* (evergreen, slow-burn, cheapest to
make badly and easiest to cut down later). Animation is spent **only** on things that cannot be
screen-recorded: the cost model, the value-flow diagram, the self-sustaining arithmetic. Everything
else is a screen, because screens are evidence and motion graphics are claims.

Three audiences, three jobs, in this order:

| Layer | Job | Videos | Why it must come in this order |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 · Proof** | Get a pilot group to say "we could use that" | V1, V2 | Without proof there is nothing to educate anyone *about* |
| **2 · Money** | Convert interest into a conversation | V3, V4 | The pain that actually moves a co-op is a line item, not a philosophy |
| **3 · Trust** | Answer "who owns this / will it exist / is it real" | V5–V8 | Objection handling — valuable, but only after the first two layers create the objection |

### V11 beat sheet — *The discussion that didn't happen*
The one video whose subject is a **habit**, not a feature. It can be filmed today because the

## 1b. The spine — one thesis underneath every episode

**Robbie's framing, 2026-09-13** (full version: `chilling-effect-and-the-story.md` §0):

> *"You don't have to be doing something morally wrong to be afraid. You just have to know you're up
> against systemic challenges, or a larger status quo poised against your direction — which is usually
> anti-cooperation and pro-monopolistic-capital."*

**The second axis, 2026-09-18 — the hidden cost is the cost of coordination:**

> *"The hidden cost this series measures is the cost of coordination itself. Coordinating with
> digital tools has a price, and that price limits how people cooperate. The experiment that is
> irl.coop depends on lowering that barrier — and then observing the metrics of cooperation (the
> regenerative and cooperativeness scores) and the changes across the verticals, in real life."*

V3, V8 and V9 are not pricing videos. They measure **this**: what coordination costs when the tools
are rented, and what changes when that cost falls.

**Every episode is a measurement of the same gradient**, which is why the series coheres:

| Episode | The part of the current it measures |
|---|---|
| V9 *The 300-user wall* | The menu ends at 300 users — growth is punished at the moment it would matter |
| V3/V8 *What this costs* | Coordination is priced — the tools of cooperation are rented, and their cost limits who cooperates |
| V11 *The discussion that didn't happen* | Legibility is the entry price — so groups self-censor before anyone acts |
| V4 *Why we're a cooperative* | Continuity is rented, and a vendor can be bought or ban you |
| V2 *One login, every tool* | Exit is expensive, so the switching cost grows with use |

**Three rules that follow, and they are also the reason this is safe to say:**

1. **Never allege a villain — show the shape.** The thesis needs no conspiracy, and that is precisely
   what makes it unanswerable. Any episode that names a perpetrator is weaker *and* less true.
2. **Name the asymmetry, not the actor.** "The menu ends at 300 users" is checkable; "they don't want
   you to grow" is not.
3. **Present every mitigation as an inversion of a default** ("here's the default, here's what we did
   instead") — which is how the cooperative identity becomes the *engineering spec* rather than a
   sentiment. See the inversion table in `chilling-effect-and-the-story.md` §0.3.

## 2. Format rules (non-negotiable, they're what make these credible)

1. **Screen > animation.** Animation only where there is no screen to show. Every animated frame is a
   claim; every recording is a receipt.
2. **Use one real group, named and consented.** Not "Acme Corp" demo data. The whole argument is
   real-world coordination; a demo tenant undercuts it in the first 10 seconds.
3. **Honesty rule — never film what doesn't work.** If a feature is designed-but-not-built, it does not
   appear in a screencast. Either build it, show it in the *design* video (V5–V6, animation, clearly
   framed as "how it's built to work"), or cut it. This is why V7 is last.
4. **Every number is date-stamped on screen** ("Slack Enterprise+, median $26.18/user/mo — Vendr, Sep
   2026"). Competitor pricing moves; the numbers must be re-verified at record time (§4).
5. **No per-seat framing.** irl.coop's differentiator is *shared infrastructure*, not a cheaper seat.
   The moment the video argues per-seat, it's competing on the incumbents' turf.
6. **V0 is the only repeatable format.** One task, one group, 90 seconds, no intro music loop, no
   "welcome back to the channel". It should be storable in one sitting and publishable same-day.

## 3. The hero videos — beat sheets

### V1 · One group, one afternoon (the proof film)

Real task, real group, single take-ish. The story arc: *a group that needs a thing done by Friday.*

1. **0:00–0:20 — the situation, stated plainly.** "Six people, one potluck-and-workshop series, no
   admin budget." No logo sting.
2. **0:20–0:50 — sign in.** Passkey, one tap. Not Google-account theatre — the point is *no new
   password, no new identity*.
3. **0:50–1:40 — the group exists.** Group page: seats, roles, who's in, who's an organizer.
4. **1:40–3:10 — make the thing.** Create the event, set a price and a capacity, publish. Then **open
   the public event page in a private window** — this is the moment the video earns trust: the page is
   public, no login wall for a ticket buyer.
5. **3:10–4:00 — a stranger buys a ticket.** Checkout, ticket issued. Cut to the group's notification
   stream: *order paid, 1 attendee, $X* — landing without anyone refreshing anything.
6. **4:00–5:00 — the group talks about it.** Chat, then the doc where the run-of-show lives.
7. **5:00–6:00 — the receipts.** Stack status page: this whole thing ran on one host, and here's what's
   declared vs running. Close on the group's own numbers: revenue collected, exports available.
8. **6:00–6:30 — the honest close.** "This is a pilot. Here's what's next and how to be one of the next
   groups." (See §5.)

### V3 · What this costs, side by side (the converter)

Structure it as three columns built up live, not a table shown at the end.

- **Column 1 — Enterprise (per-seat, negotiated).** The honest version of this tier: it's not the list
  price, it's the *procurement process*. Slack Enterprise+ is custom-quoted; real purchases land around
  **$26.18/user/mo median** (Vendr, 535 verified purchases). At 40 people that's ~$12.6k/yr for chat
  alone — before the docs tool, the project tool, the forms tool, the event tool, the survey tool, each
  of which is another per-seat line. **The point isn't that it's expensive; it's that cost scales with
  headcount while a co-op's budget scales with members who can't always pay.**
- **Column 2 — Mid-tier fixed monthly (per-org).** The honest version: this is the tier most co-ops
  actually live in, and it's genuinely good value. Anchors: **Circle ~$89/mo** entry, **Mighty Networks
  ~$95/mo** entry, **Wild Apricot ~$48–$54/mo for 100 contacts → ~$350/mo at 50,000 contacts**. Fixed
  fee — but the fee is fixed *and the ceiling is fixed*: contact-count tiers, per-app fees, seats you
  can't add without paying.
- **Column 3 — irl.coop.** The honest version: membership, not license. Infrastructure is shared across
  every group on the node, so the marginal group costs ~nothing to add (§4). What a group pays for is
  *stewardship*, not seats. No contact-count ceiling, no per-app fee, and the code is the same code
  running for everyone.

**The closing comparison is not "we're cheaper".** It's: *at 40 members, the commercial stack runs
~$3,281/mo — and the per-seat column is doing most of that work at **$2,318.86/mo** — while one
shared node runs on **$77.50/mo** of running cost. The groups can export everything and leave with
it.*

**Work these four examples on camera** (they're the ones with teeth — full framing in
`cost-model.md` → "The four comparisons worth doing on camera"):

- **Accounting/QuickBooks** — the cleanest cost argument. Per-company subscription, $38 → $340
  spread, Plus jumped **$99 → $140** in Aug 2026. Show `accounting.irl.coop` actually running.
- **Telephony** — a *licence* argument, not a free-numbers argument. Name the carrier DID price
  out loud ($1.50/mo × 3 illustrate) so nobody can accuse the model of hiding it; then the real
  point: carriers sell a line **per person**, the node rents numbers **per group**.
- **Storage** — **measured on the host, and the measurement is the credibility**: 34.3 GB on disk,
  only 11.25 GB live, the 10 GB basemap stored **twice**, 12.9 GB of reproducible build input, and
  postgres excluded because the service account can't read it. Then the rate ($0.00217 vs S3
  $0.023/GB/mo) — and the honest pivot to **egress**, where S3's $0.09/GB turns 200 GB/mo of
  shared event video into **$18/mo of transfer**. Concede the 87 GB capacity ceiling yourself.
- **Maps** — **do not claim a saving.** At 25k loads/month both Mapbox (50k free) and Google
  (10k free) swallow the traffic and a co-op pays $0 either way. The segment is *terms*: no card
  on file, no re-pricing, no rate-limits, no switch-off. Explicitly conceding the money here buys
  credibility for every other number in the video.

### V9 · The 300-user wall (the flagship argument)

This is the most shareable piece in the set, and the only one that is *aimed at a belief* rather
than a capability. Its job: make a small org realise the reason it never attempted scale was a
menu that ends, not a price it couldn't afford.

1. **0:00–0:40 — the felt experience first, no data.** "Every small org has had this
   conversation: we could do something much bigger — and then someone says *we couldn't manage
   it*, and everyone nods." Recognition before evidence. The nod is what the video is about.
2. **0:40–1:30 — the wall, on screen.** Three vendors, ~250–300 users: Microsoft's **hard** 300
   ceiling across the whole Business family (documented, not a guideline), Google's 300,
   Zoom's 250, Slack's ~500. Show what happens at 301: the product you were buying stops
   existing and you're in procurement. "You have met this wall before. Probably more than once."
3. **1:30–3:00 — the arithmetic at 10,000.** Identity $140k, chat $110k, docs $30k, mail $15k on
   the cheapest *uncapped* SKU → **$295,000/mo, $3.54M/yr** — and that's a **floor** (project
   management has no published price at that size at all). Then the line that lands:
   **participation IS the bill** — Slack prices per monthly active user, so a movement's growth
   is its invoice.
4. **3:00–3:45 — concede the nonprofit programs, unprompted.** Google Workspace free for
   eligible 501(c)(3); Airtable $12 vs $20; Microsoft's grants. Then the two limits: eligibility
   is organisational, and these cover **staff accounts, not 10,000 volunteers**. Doing this
   *before* the audience does is the credibility hinge of the entire series.
5. **3:45–4:30 — what orgs actually do instead.** Discord, WhatsApp, Facebook Groups, a Google
   Group, a spreadsheet. Free at scale because somebody else's model pays. Reframe the choice:
   funder-backed extractive, or free-but-ungoverned-and-inexportable. A movement that wants
   neither currently has nothing — that's the gap.
6. **4:30–5:30 — the same movement on a node.** $2,026/mo at 10k, $6,024/mo at 50k. The curve
   *bends the right way*: **$2.43 → $1.45 per participant per year** as it grows. Then the
   honest limits, out loud: not one box, mail deliverability is real work, and **coordination
   labour is the binding constraint** — the platform changes *who decides* to staff it, not
   whether it's work.
7. **5:30–6:00 — the close.** "The wall isn't a price. It's a menu that ends at 300 people.
   We're not cheaper per seat — we're the option that doesn't stop selling to you."

### V10 · Run a node (the recruitment piece)

**Blocked on one real thing:** nobody has stood up a second node yet. The honesty rule wins —
you cannot screencast a deployment that has only been designed. The unblock is small and
concrete: **stand up one edge node** (nginx + MinIO + a backup target, ~5 apps) on a Pi or a
$6 VPS, holding a replica of the shared assets and serving the basemap for one region. That is
phase 1 of the on-ramp, and standing it up *is* the demonstration.

Once it exists, the video is a screencast of the real thing:

1. **0:00–0:20 — the ask, immediately.** "One hour. Six dollars a month. No server rack."
   State the whole offer before explaining anything.
2. **0:20–1:00 — why it matters.** The federation's redundancy isn't funded — it's the
   by-product of nodes existing. Each replica is **2 cents a month** of idle disk; the same
   three replicas on S3 are $0.78/mo, and the shape is what counts.
3. **1:00–2:00 — do it, on screen.** Real terminal: pull the edge profile, bring it up, point it
   at the federation's assets, watch a region's map tiles get served. One hour of work, filmed
   in three minutes.
4. **2:00–2:30 — the honest part, before the invitation.** *Nobody is paged at 2am. If your
   node dies the federation loses a replica, not its data — but the promise stops at the asset
   layer; there is no uptime number.* Then the ladder: add a backup target ($0), take a pillar,
   graduate to a full instance.
5. **2:30–3:00 — the close, aimed at the org not the individual.** "You get a governance seat,
   your data on hardware you control, and no per-seat bill. You're not donating compute — you're
   taking a seat at the table."

### V8 · Why this can pay for itself

This is the strongest asset in the set **because it is arithmetic, not promise** — and it's the one video
a skeptic can verify. Show the actual model (§4): fixed cost of the node, split across N groups, and the
number of groups at which the node covers its own hosting. Then the open-source half: every app in the
stack is software the co-op didn't pay to write (Keycloak, Synapse/Element, Stalwart, Plane, NocoDB,
MinIO, Traefik, Postgres) — the co-op pays for *stewardship of it*, not for licences.

Rule for this video: **only measured numbers**, pulled from the running stack, with the command shown.
No TAM slides.

## 4. The cost model (build this once; V3 + V8 both consume it)

**Built** (2026-09-13): `docs/design/cost-model.md` (narrative) +
`docs/design/cost-model.data.json` (every price with a source and a verification date) +
`infra/scripts/cost-model.py` (stdlib emitter, `--inject` refreshes the doc). The emitter
**refuses to put an unverified price into a total** — unverified entries are listed as open
items instead — so the model cannot drift into marketing. Never hand-type a number into a video.

Three blocks:

**A. The node's real cost** — what this host + router + DNS + certs + backups actually cost per month.
Partly measurable now (host cost is yours to state; storage/compute are local). This is the denominator
for everything else.

**B. The stack's replacement value** — for each pillar, the commercial equivalent and its list cost:
chat (Slack/Teams), mail+calendar (Google Workspace / M365), project mgmt (Asana/Plane), databases
(NocoDB ↔ Airtable), docs+storage (Drive/Dropbox), events+ticketing (Eventbrite — note its fee is
*per ticket*, not per month, which is a different shape of argument), forms/surveys (Typeform),
identity (Okta — per-seat, and this is the one nobody thinks about), video calls (Zoom).
All **48 comparables are verified** with per-price source and date in `cost-model.data.json` —
**zero open items**. Headline shape at the current scenario (**40 members**, 6 organizers,
$4k/mo ticket revenue, 25k map loads, 200 GB/mo egress): **per-seat `standard` $2,318.86/mo**
(9 slices — 3 charged to all 40 members: identity, mail, chat; 6 charged at the 6 organizers,
incl. telephony) · **mid-tier fixed fees $340.54/mo across 7 subscriptions** (not one, incl.
QuickBooks Essentials) · **ticketing platform fees $622/mo — 15.6% of the group's own
revenue** · **map tiles $0** · **object storage $0.08** · **egress $0** → **commercial total
$3,281.48/mo** against **$77.50/mo** of node running cost ($73 infrastructure + $4.50 of
carrier numbers it can't make free; $437.50 with stewardship). Ratios: **45×**
infrastructure-only, **42×** like-for-like running cost, **8×** honest. Prices are list, dated
2026-09-13, and must be re-verified at record time.

**Four slices each argue differently** (full framing in `cost-model.md` → "The four
comparisons worth doing on camera"):

- **Accounting/QuickBooks** — the cleanest cost argument. Per-company, $38 → $340 spread, Plus
  jumped **$99 → $140** in Aug 2026, vs ERPNext already live at `accounting.irl.coop`.
- **Telephony** — a *licence* argument, not a free-numbers argument. Name the carrier DID price
  ($1.50/mo × 3) so nobody can say it was hidden; the real point is that carriers sell a line
  **per person** while the node rents numbers **per group**.
- **Maps** — **do not claim a saving.** At 25k loads/month both Mapbox (50k free) and Google
  (10k free) swallow the traffic. The segment is *terms*.
- **Storage** — **measured on the host** (34.3 GB total, 11.25 GB live; 12.9 GB is reproducible
  build input and the 10 GB basemap is stored **twice**). Node rate **$0.00217/GB/mo** vs S3
  **$0.023** — but that is 2¢ vs 26¢, so the money is not the point either. The points are: one
  shared pool instead of a quota per member, and **egress**, where S3's **$0.09/GB** makes
  200 GB/mo of shared video **$18/mo in transfer alone**. Concede the capacity ceiling (87 GB
  free vs Google's 2 TB *per user*) before someone else raises it.

**C. The self-sustaining line** — the crossover point. Fixed node cost ÷ cost-per-active-group ≈ N
groups to break even; then the honest caveats (support labour is the real cost, not compute; the node
has a single-host failure domain; growth in members ≠ growth in cost). Showing the caveats is what makes
the video credible to the exact audience that has been burned by a platform folding before.

## 5. The launch-strategy video (honest version)

The instinct is a "we're launching!" video. Don't — nobody is waiting for a launch, and a launch video
that nobody watches becomes evidence against you. Film the **strategy** instead, as a note to the
sector: *how we're bringing this online.*

Beats: **few select groups → staged testing (what's live, what's not) → real-work marketing.** Name the
phases. State what's live today, what's designed, and what's parked. Say plainly that the pilot group
count is small on purpose, and why. Then the invitation: *bring us a group that has a task to get done
by a date.*

This video doubles as the pilot-recruitment page for the landing site — it *is* the funnel entry, so
make it V0-adjacent in tone (plain, concrete, no anthem).

Also film **V0 as a series from the pilot groups themselves** — "getting things done in the real world"
is exactly the recurring 90-second format, and it's content the sector generates for you.

## 5b. Sector hooks — one sentence each

V9 and V10 need per-audience cuts, and the sector analysis
(`adversary-models-and-sector-fit.md` §4) produces the copy directly. Each sector has exactly one
sentence that lands, because each has a different adversary. Use the sentence, then show the
mechanism that makes it true:

| Sector | The sentence | The mechanism behind it |
|---|---|---|
| **Mutual aid** | "No one can produce a list of who got help." | non-enumeration + unlinkable donor↔recipient |
| **Worker unions (organizing)** | "Organize before anyone can be listed." | hidden-tier commitments, provable-not-enumerable membership |
| **Worker unions (recognized)** | — the protection *shrinks by law* once the unit is disclosed. Say so. | mode flip `hidden → members` |
| **Advocacy** | "The strategy is not in the same place as the newsletter." | least privilege + explicit public/private split |
| **Community safety** | "The people in the room can't see the room's records." | compartmentalization, separate DEKs per compartment |
| **Rave / subculture** | "There is no list to seize." | Deniable mode: no central roster, short-lived keys |
| **Entheogenic / alt-health** | "The practitioner holds the key — not the platform, not the node, not the group." | per-practitioner custody (and the honest "cannot be exculpated" trade) |
| **Food hubs / adoption co-ops** | "They can count your trucks. They can't read your plan." | footprint Sparse/Unreadable; encrypted supplier terms and volumes |
| **Identity-threatened food coalitions** | "Nothing here can be used to find your members." | non-enumeration as *safety*, per-pair visibility |
| **Certified organic operations** | "You can prove it to the certifier without handing the map to anyone else." | selective disclosure vs 7 CFR Part 205 traceability |
| **Land / housing** | "The rule that says you can't sell it is written where you can't edit it." | constitutional governance, timelocks |
| **Tool libraries, childcare rotas, public events** | "Nobody pays per seat for a volunteer rota." | Open mode, scoped to *the parts that are meant to be public* — not to whole sectors (§4J of the adversary analysis records why generalising that was wrong) |

Two rules for these videos, derived from the same analysis:

- **Never claim protection at the physical layer.** A phone seized at a rave with the passcode given
  under pressure is not a platform problem, and implying otherwise is the most common dishonest
  claim in privacy marketing. Say the limit before the audience finds it.
- **Pick the sector's *own* adversary for the threat story.** A mutual-aid audience does not fear
  hackers; it fears the person who knows where they live. Getting that wrong makes the whole
  platform sound like it was built for somebody else.

## 7. Distribution (the part most strategies skip)

This is not a YouTube-discovery play; the audience is narrow and reachable directly.

- **Publish:** the irl.coop site (a `/stories` page holding V0 + V1), plus platform.coop, co-op
  developer networks, r/cooperatives, regional co-op associations, and directly in the wallets of
  sector newsletters.
- **Send V1 to** organizers who already have a coordination problem and no budget — not to developers.
- **Send V3 to** anyone who has ever signed a SaaS renewal for a volunteer organization.
- **Never lead with V5/V6.** The "what is a platform co-op" video is for people who arrived via V1 or
  V3 and want the deeper why. Leading with education is how projects get a thousand views and no groups.
