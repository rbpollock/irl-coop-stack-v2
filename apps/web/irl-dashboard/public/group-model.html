Technical Overview · Design Reference

The Group as the Primitive: Safe-Based Membership, Declarative Group Shapes, and Provable Governance

A consolidated account of the cooperative group model underlying irl.coop — its identity layer, data model, provisioning machinery, on-chain mechanisms, zero-knowledge proof system, and application integration points.

Author: irl.coop (Robbie Pollock) Status: design + partial implementation Version: 2026-09-04

Abstract

irl.coop is organized around a single primitive: the group. Rather than a separate "organization" entity layered on top of user accounts, a group is a Safe smart contract — the same ERC-4337 account a person holds, deployed with an N-of-M owner set instead of a 1-of-1 passkey. Membership is the seat set; the narrower on-chain owner set is governance, and where the two differ is the shape a group can move over its life (§9.1). A Citus projection renders both for applications. Every application consumes the group as its own tenant object through a single indirection row, resource_scopes {group_id, app, resource_key}, and a per-request identity variable, app.sub, enforced by Postgres row-level security. Groups are born from a five-axis declarative shape (roles, apps, config, governance, proofs), assembled by composition rather than inheritance, and — by default — hidden: membership is provable in zero-knowledge but never enumerable. A central event bus (source → event → notification → channel) and a Temporal provisioning reconciler tie the model together, with a private shielded treasury, ERC-7715 permission-grant delegation, and a ZK regenerative-score metric as the economic and reputational spine.

1  Introduction

The design ambition of irl.coop is a cooperative in which the group — not the user, and not an "organization" entity — is the fundamental unit of membership, governance, and application tenancy. This document consolidates the current design and the implemented subset into one reference: what the group is, how membership is represented and proven, how groups are provisioned, and how every application in the stack attaches to them.

Three commitments recur throughout and should be read as the load-bearing invariants:

  • The group is the primitive, not the user. Every account is already a Safe; the co-op is one Safe the members hold together. There is no second account type.
  • Projection, never truth. The chain (the Safe's owner set and threshold) is the source of truth. Everything else — seats, resources, the world-doc — is a rebuildable projection of that on-chain fact.
  • Hidden is the norm. Most groups are hidden by default; membership is provable on demand but never enumerable by third parties.

2  The group primitive

A group is a Safe smart contract. An individual account is a 1-of-1 Safe (a single passkey owner); a group is the same contract deployed with an N-of-M owner set. The cooperative is expressed as a graph:

nodes (Safe-accounts) + edges (memberships) = one cooperative graph. — irl-coop-group.md, Principle 1

The model is split into two layers:

LayerRoleWhereProperty
Layer 1 — truthowner set + threshold; who can acton-chain (Safe)authoritative, provable
Layer 2 — projectionqueryable registry (members, seats, scoped resources)Citus irlcooprebuildable from Layer 1; "dies nothing when dropped"

Groups compose because a Safe can be an owner of another Safe — so a child group is simply another Safe seated in the parent's owner set, with no special machinery. irl.coop is one such group: a peer that stewards the technology stack and redistributes funds (§A.4), not a root above other groups. Its Safe address is derived from a fixed well-known salt keccak256("irl.coop"), a platform constant that survives secret rotation.

3  Seats & membership

The seat is the universal membership primitive — the per-(user, group) persona. A seat is a membership, with presentation attached:

seat = { sub          — Keycloak realm subject (private, never rendered)
         alias        — "Harvest Coordinator" | "Robert"   (presentation in THIS group)
         roles        — [...]
         visibility   — "role-only" | "alias" | "canonical"
         MatrixId     — @<localpart>:matrix.irl.coop
         joinedAt }

Because every application reads the same seat record, the same person is "Robert" in one group and "Coordinator" in another, and a governance action ("approve as member of Cold Storage Co-op — 2-of-3") renders identically in mail, a Matrix room, and a project. The personal account is the "self-group" seat; the group seat is the first-class citizen. "Every app reads the seat, never the raw identity."

Two distinct enums are easily conflated and worth separating precisely:

Hidden membership is commitment-based: the facts (member-of, subgroup-of, sponsored-by, distance) are attested by zk-badges on demand, never enumerated. The plaintext group_members projection remains the substrate only for open/members groups.

4  Roles & capabilities

Two role vocabularies coexist and are reconciled in the implementation:

  • Design model (irl-coop-group.md): founder, admin, member, inviter, observer — "labels on owner sets," stored in the seat index, governed by the Safe.
  • Implemented RBAC (coop_rls.sql): owner (4), admin (3), inviter (2), member (1), observer (0) — where owner is the implemented counterpart of the design's founder. Ownership is a seat with role 'owner'.

Roles are bundles of grants — a capability catalog resolved through three catalog tables (roles, grants, role_grants). The capability check coop_has_grant(gid, g) resolves a role name through role_grants.grant_name. The live grant catalog:

App-layer enforcement of capabilities is a UX gate; "the Safe is the final arbiter (EIP-1271 for signatures)."

5  Identity, keys & admission

5.1  Invite-on-first-signin

There is no separate "invite" concept in development — "the Google account IS the invitation." The first sign-in through the Google broker / Keycloak atomically: (a) creates the person account (their own 1-of-1 Safe), and (b) provisions their Matrix account and personal space. Signing up does not seat anyone in irl.coop — joining the coop is a separate, opt-in act (§A.4). ensureGroupSeat is fully idempotent — "a convergent function, not a one-shot job." The admission gate is a group setting: open, invite-list (Safe-controlled allowlist), or approval (a pending seat an inviter approves).

5.2  Deterministic addresses (CREATE2)

Every Safe address is CREATE2-deterministic from a user-held saltNonce; the proxy salt is keccak256(abi.encodePacked(keccak256(initializer), saltNonce)), so the initializer (owners/threshold) is part of the salt and no identity→onchain mapping is stored server-side ("uncorrelatable Safe provisioning"). Salt variants:

  • user-created groups: random freshSalt()
  • personal account: sub-derived deterministic salt
  • irl.coop (the steward group): fixed keccak256("irl.coop")
  • co-op group Safe (design): fixed documented salt irlcoop-group-v1

5.3  Passkeys & session keys

Passkey-first, with a deliberate concealment of the machinery: "members must never see keys, thresholds, or the word 'Safe' if we can avoid it." An individual is 1-of-1; a user Safe's proposed ownership is 2-of-3 (passkey + user recovery + the DAO Safe as assist-only owner). P-256 passkey verification runs through the Base precompile 0x00…0100 per EIP-7212. Routine operations run under session keys (the SessionKeyModule) for a small blast radius.

Delegation, not custody is the settled invariant. Delegation is expressed as an ERC-7715 permission grant (Safe → signer) with three properties: (1) the signer is a permitted key, not an owner; (2) the permission is scoped (bounded actions, caps, recipient allowlists, expiry); (3) revocation is instant and on-chain, controlled by the Safe. One primitive, three holder-shapes — an interactive session key (user device), an automation/delivery key (platform-custodial, narrow per-workflow), and an agent key. A role is itself "a named bundle of scoped ERC-7715 grants, not a string": assigning a role is the group Safe signing the grants to the member's key; removing it is revocation. The delegation registry enumerates every delegate with scope and expiry; the Safe is the revocation authority for everything.

No bypass on Temporal. Every Temporal call is authorized by a user's ERC-4337 session key. No service identity, no system role, no BYPASSRLS function, no cross-user reader. — delegation-and-session-keys.md, settled invariant

6  Governance

Voting is Safe transactions with a UX layer: propose (any member creates a proposal with title, options, quorum %, deadline, optional transaction payload) → deliberate (announced to the governance Matrix room) → vote (members sign the proposal hash with EIP-1271 — the personal Safe's signature) → count (quorum % of the owner set by deadline) → execute (a Safe transaction, or a recorded on-chain signed statement). The deterministic vote id is H(groupId, nonce, proposerSub).

Group-shape governance splits the meta-axis into constitutional (amendment rules, quorum, admission/expulsion — hard to change) and ordinary (app bundle, delivery prefs, role assignments — shifted under the constitutional process). A split is a constitutional event producing two new group records seeded from the parent's current state, not the original shape.

Governance is binary — a Safe address is or is not in the elected owner set; membership is a seat, and the boundary between the two is the group's moveable shape (§9.1). "ownership implies membership ONLY — a Safe in another's owner-set is a seat in its decisions, nothing more." Reserved powers are not a special owner role but a co-owner seat + allowance/session-key module + guard module + timelock guard. Relationship edges are typed over four dimensions — Governance (owner seats · threshold), Infrastructure (namespace · mailboxes · quotas), Trust (OIDC · SMTP · Matrix · arbitration), Economics (funding · sponsorship) — yielding the relationship types member-of, subgroup-of, federated-with, sponsored-by.

7  Data scoping & projection

The projection enforces visibility in Postgres, "not the app — no app-level bypass." Identity is app.sub, a per-request session variable set by coop-api. FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY plus a BYPASSRLS helper role (coop_rls) make policies fire even for the table-owner connection.

The RLS predicate helpers (SECURITY DEFINER, owned by coop_rls):

  • coop_current_sub() — reads current_setting('app.sub', true)
  • coop_is_member(gid) / coop_is_owner(gid) / coop_is_creator(gid)
  • coop_can_view_group(gid) — privacy = 'open' OR coop_is_member OR created_by = current_sub
  • coop_ensure_personal_group() — idempotently provisions the "self-group"
  • coop_has_grant(gid, g) — role→grant bundle check

resource_scopes is the universal link row: every group-scoped resource — a Matrix room, a Plane project, a NocoDB base, a MinIO key prefix, a FreeSWITCH DID, a Postiz org, a LiteFarm farm — is one row carrying app + resource_key + scoped_by. The per-user projection is a materialized view family (world_doc__%SUB_SANITIZED%, deterministically named) that unions "my groups" with "resources scoped to my groups" — a join over group_members → resource_scopes → per-app projections, group-scoped rather than merely per-user.

8  Communications

Communications are derived, not stored. Each group gets a Matrix space + channel set created by coop-api through the Synapse admin API (POST /_synapse/admin/v1/rooms): a space plus general, governance, treasury, and per-project rooms, with deterministic aliases (#irl-coop-general:matrix.irl.coop). Power levels map roles: founder/admin = 100, member = 50, observer = 0 (read-only). Group privacy maps to room access: open → public/public/listed, members → private/invite/unlisted, hidden → private/invite/unlisted. The room list is rebuilt from the Synapse admin API on every world-doc refresh — "no second registry of channels."

9  Group shapes

A shape is the template a group is born from — five axes, four inward and one outward:

Table 4 — the five-axis group shape seed
AxisContent
rolesthe vocabulary shared between authorization and notification targeting
appswhat gets provisioned, each with a hook
configdefault delivery/urgency rules
governancethresholds, quotas, membership policy — the meta-axis (constitutional vs ordinary)
proofsthe outward interface: what the group can reveal without exposing internals

Shape = seed, not cage. All five axes are initial values written to the group's Postgres record at provisioning; afterwards the group's record is authoritative and the shape is never re-read. Composition, not inheritance: shapes assemble primitives rather than extend a base ("Men's circle" is a custom composition, not a subclass of "group"), so a built-in shape evolving later never silently changes existing groups. Three lifecycles: built-in (curated, git), custom (per-group, Postgres), and shared (a group exports its shape to a public blueprint library; others adopt/remix). The library shares structure (roles, apps, governance, proof schema), never records (members, votes, metrics) — a blueprint commons, not a data commons.

Child relationships are a rights set, not a flag. A child group is an autonomous Safe holding a subgroup-of edge (governance + infrastructure: shares the parent's namespace, terms declare veto/reserved-powers/autonomy) and possibly a sponsored-by edge (economics: the funding allocation with caps and budget-transfer authority on the edge). The child is a full group — own Safe, own treasury, own seats and governance; strip the binding and it is independent. A sub-project becomes a child group by promotion, not subclassing.

9.1  Shape & lifecycle — the group as a living thing

A group is not a fixed type; it is a living shape. A project — with one or many people and a shared aim — can graduate through a ceremony into a group, declaring its starting shape and its cadence. From there a group grows: it promotes a child, merges with a peer, devolves a slice, splits in two. The platform's real work is carrying these flows and transitions. 10  Event bus & provisioning

10.1  The event bus

The bus is a many-to-many router: sources emit events, channels deliver them. The pipeline is source → event → notification → channel, where an event is raw and typed (vote.cast, match.found, mail.received) and a notification is rendered, user-scoped, localized. Persisting raw events (rather than collapsing to notifications at publish) yields three things free: watches (a subscription layer over the stream), Temporal (a workflow is just another channel), and future sources.

Group targets resolve server-side — coop-api looks up members + roles, filters by a role allowlist, and publishes to each eligible member's channel; the client only ever subscribes to its own channel and never does role filtering. Delivery is not the bus's job: the bus decides who should know; a Temporal workflow decides what to do about it (the bus never learns SMTP or Matrix). Anonymization is owned by the source.

10.2  Provisioning

A group template (per-shape YAML declaring the app bundle) maps 1:1 onto a Temporal provisioning workflow: each declared app is an activity (hook), run in order, idempotent, with compensation on failure. Provisioned state (what this group actually got) is stored in Postgres. The reconciler is:

declared  = catalog features × this group's activation    (what it should have)
actual    = resource_scopes + per-app state               (what it got)
drift     = declared − actual                             (missing → provision it)

Activation rows live in app_activations(group_id, app, enabled, adopted_at), kept separate from the shape so the shape stays read-only and the sweep can index by app. Adoption policy: core features opt-out (batch-reconciled on ship), optional apps opt-in (lazy, hook fires on first use), platform always (declarative build). The reconciler never rewrites customization — composition, not inheritance.

11  On-chain mechanisms & the private treasury

The treasury is private by default and pure-math: "no TEE, no trusted operator — all hiding/proving is ZK (commitments, proofs, nullifiers)." Every account (person, group, DAO) holds exactly one private treasury, bound to its Safe but logically separate from governance state. The chain stores no balances — only commitments:

  • Notes (UTXOs). commit(amount, owner_key, project_scope, rng) — a note is an asset of exactly one project account inside one treasury.
  • Commitments tree. Each treasury's notes form a Merkle tree; the root is the chain state. Balances are unreadable — only commitments.
  • Nullifiers. Spending reveals nullifier = H(note_secret); the chain enforces "nullifier unseen," so a note is spent exactly once — double-counting structurally impossible.
  • Transfers as ZK proofs. A transfer proves conservation in-circuit (inputs = outputs), existence of each input nullifier, authorization (the Safe's quorum signed via EIP-1271), and new output commitments.
  • Viewing keys. A master viewing key (custodian set) and per-project project viewing keys open only that project's notes — compliance is a scope-limited report, never the whole book.

Guards gate everything at three enforcement points — (1) in-circuit conditions (the transfer proof attests policy: per-ledger limits, required seats as the Safe's signature in public inputs, timelock windows, zk-badge gates), (2) the on-chain TransactionGuard (authoritative for governance-adjacent actions: owner-set changes, module installs, relationship-record edits), and (3) app-layer pre-validation (coop-api's policy engine, never authoritative alone). Guards are data-driven, versioned, and upgradeable only through the guarded path.

The ledger taxonomy splits value into three integrity tiers:

1money: savings, shares, capital, distributions, DAO fundsshielded on-chain (ZK treasury)conservation in-circuit + nullifiers + consensus

Settlement and sponsorship are chargeback-aware. Fiat is reversible ~130 days; Safe (crypto) is final. A sponsor is a vertical fund — a subgroup-of irl.coop whose Safe is the fund's treasury. A group is sponsored-by its fund (one-way flow, recognition, no control), with cap, settlement fee, and repayment schedule as terms on the edge. The T+130 settlement is the only visible crossing; chargeback losses are absorbed by the fund's reserve sub-account up to the per-vertical cap.

12  Zero-knowledge proving

The group graph is hidden by default, provable on demand. Four statements of increasing difficulty:

Table 6 — proof statements and constructions
StatementConstructionFeasibility
Membership — "X ∈ members(G)"Merkle proof over the group's member-commitment tree (Semaphore / zk-kit)off-the-shelf
Role — "X holds Y in G"membership proof with the role inside the leaf commitmentoff-the-shelf
Relationship — "edge (G₁,R,G₂)"membership proof over an edge-commitment tree (reveal R + one endpoint)off-the-shelf
Non-membership — "X ∉ members(G)"frontier tree, or sorted-tree range proof (Micali–Rabin–Kilian ZK-Sets)off-the-shelf
Bounded distance — "dist(X,G) ≥ K" (K small)conjunction of non-membership over G's ≤(K−1)-hop neighborhoodfeasible, medium effort
Exact distance — "dist(X,G) = K"ZKGraph-style SSSP verification, O(m) circuitresearch-grade

Each group maintains a member commitment tree whose leaves are commit(sub, role) (the Keycloak sub + seat roles, blinded by a per-seat nonce); membership is a standard Merkle-authentication-path proof in ZK so neither sub nor role is revealed beyond the statement. Decision (recommended): ship bounded distance with K ∈ {1,2,3} (the form actually needed for conflict-of-interest exclusion); defer exact distance indefinitely. Prover runs client-side (sovereignty pattern; witnesses never leave the device); verifier runs on-chain (Groth16/PLONK over Poseidon-on-BN254); coop-api is the relay.

Two tiers of "hidden" — tier 1 (hidden-from-public, the default): membership stored centrally but RLS-scoped, no public projection, roles minted into the JWT as today; tier 2 (hidden-from-platform, opt-in/deferred): the platform holds only commitment roots, the user presents membership proofs at sign-in, and member fan-out runs on derived delivery keys (D_i = H(sub_i, group_id) · G, stealth-address style) so endpoints are enumerable but unlinkable to identities.

The regenerative score is a ZK-metric over four generic-core signals — flow (value actually moved), reciprocity (balanced vs extractive), distribution (spread vs hub-concentrated), persistence (retention + succession) — each required to pass an inclusion test that is provable in ZK and hard to game. A proof binds a timestamp/block ("as of block N, score ≥ 0.8") and the group's public identity so it can't be replayed. Honest over comprehensive: a Sybil-friendly metric defeats the proof, since a ZK proof vouches for the claim, never the truth of the data.

14  Integration workflows

Integration workflows are the backbone of the system's operation: how actions are implemented across multiple apps, and how provable private records are created that make sense of what has happened — allowing complex execution and communication steps to branch from a single event.

One row, one event, one workflow. Every app object is keyed to the group by a resource_scopes row; every change is a typed event on the bus; Temporal listens and translates. Apps never call each other directly — Plane emits project.completed, coop-api mints the proposal, the keeper moves the money.

Threaded example — "buy a cold frame": a task with a $320 estimate → a proposal → 3-of-5 signs with EIP-1271 → execute is a guarded transfer from the operating fund → the task flips to funded → project.completed → the calendar clears → members are notified.

The same rule covers every app, not just Plane: a Formbricks response feeds social.*; a NocoDB row is a reservation; a Matrix message is a chat.* event. Integration is one pattern, many instances — every app-to-app flow is this pattern with a different row and a different event.

16  Tensions & open problems

  • Two role vocabularies coexist (design founder/admin/member/inviter/observer vs implemented owner/admin/inviter/member/observer, owner ↔ founder); the reconciliation should be made explicit and one vocabulary retired.
  • Two delegation terms appear across docs — "ERC-4337 session key" (§2 settled invariant) and "ERC-7715 permission grant" (§3/§4) — preserved here verbatim but in need of a single canonical name.
  • Exact graph distance ("dist(X,G) = K") is research-grade (ZKGraph lineage, O(m) circuit); the recommended shipped form is bounded distance K ∈ {1,2,3}.
  • Hidden-from-platform (tier 2) is deferred: it requires JWT mint to become proof-verification and fan-out to move to derived delivery keys — per-group opt-in, not yet the norm.
  • Matrix room→group scoping is pending: events currently land in the sender's personal group until a room is scoped to its group.
  • Federation (library hosting, cross-domain SSO/BYOD) is parked by design; the group model is local-first.
  • Prover performance on mobile is the single real risk flagged in the ZK feasibility verdict — cryptographic possibility is not in question.

Addendum — Worked Examples: Group Shapes in Practice

This addendum grounds the abstractions above in a set of shared pathways — the recurring flows the example shapes compose from — and draws out one consequence the model was designed for: many small groups sharing a single website, while money still settles to individuals. Throughout, "project management" = Plane, "visual database" = NocoDB, "storage" = MinIO, "chat" = Matrix/Cinny, and "website builder" = Webstudio.

A.1  The shared pathways

Shapes are not bespoke — each is a bundle of a small set of shared pathways. Where the main body describes what a group is and how it is provisioned, this section names the nine recurring flows that do the actual work. A shape selects which pathways apply; that selection is the shape — composition made literal. Each pathway is drawn below as a flow, with the examples that apply. The worked deep-dives (A.2, A.6, A.7) show specific shapes; here the point is that every shape is just a selection of these pathways.

1. Reserve — the physical commons

A member declares a shared thing (tool, plot, clothes, a watch slot); another reserves it with a due-back; the reservation ledger is the whole economy, so no money changes hands.

2. Match — exchange

Two offers cross the matcher (manure↔hay, need→solution, dancer↔event); a match.found event settles them as Tier-2 credits or introduces the parties.

3. Credit — reciprocity

Hours and contributions are recorded off-chain and hash-chained; balances trend to zero, and the fairness signal (the regenerative score) flags who never reciprocates.

4. Fund & distribute — money

Inflows (dues, tithes, tickets, prize) land in the group's Safe — the flat pool; distributions auto-split per the entitlement table via the keeper; project viewing keys scope compliance.

5. Govern — decisions

Propose → deliberate → members sign with EIP-1271 → count quorum → execute (a Safe transaction or a recorded on-chain statement).

6. Cover — duty & rota

A coverage constraint (three-on-watch, one kayaker per three swimmers) → a rotation schedule → coverage is provable, and the constraint is a guard: an event cannot be scheduled "safe" unless it is covered.

7. Anonymize — privacy

Submit under a pen name (or a commitment); read, judge, and critique anonymously; attribution is revealed only at the end — or never. Anonymization is owned by the source.

8. Prove — credentials & membership

A quorum issues a badge (a committed leaf); the holder range-proves "≥ passing" without revealing grade or identity; presents it with a nullifier (one slot per event); verifies against a public root.

9. Curate & federate — discovery

Local events are curated (curator-picked + community-signaled) into a projection; locale groups subgroup-of a parent so one site unions every region's list.

A.3  One website, many groups, money to individuals

The example shapes are hyper-local by design: a single city block might host the growing team, the tool library, the childcare co-op, and the hand-me-downs circle at once. Two existing edges do the work.

1. subgroup-of — one website. The block is a group; each activity is a child group sharing its namespace. One site at {block}.irl.coop renders every child under the parent's projection. The children remain full groups — each with its own Safe, treasury, seats, and governance — so "ownership implies membership ONLY": the parent is a namespace host, not a landlord. A child that outgrows the block takes its site, its Safe, and its money with it (strip the binding and it is independent).

2. The flat funding pool — money to individuals. Each group's Safe is its funding pool (there is no separate entity). Money reaches individuals through the treasury's project accounts:

  • Distribution fund — "auto-split per entitlement table, monthly via keeper." The twenty hands that harvest get their share per the entitlement table, not by a treasurer deciding each time.
  • Tier-2 labor credit — the in-kind economy (childcare turns, harvest-paid-in-food, teaching hours) is off-chain hash-chained, so "everyone gets a turn" is auditable without forcing money into a non-monetary exchange.
  • Project viewing keys — compliance is scope-limited: a vertical fund sees "the growing team distributed $X this month," never each household's balance.
  • T+130 settlement — when real fiat crosses (course fees, ticket sales, material pools), the vertical fund absorbs chargeback exposure while the group's crypto stays final.

The consequence is the design point the examples were chosen to illustrate: the website is shared but the money is not. A single site renders N groups; each group's Safe settles its own members independently. Sharing a website never implies sharing a treasury.

A.4  Stewardship and funding: the coop's verticals

The verticals are not a hierarchy rooted at irl.coop — they are irl.coop's funding priorities. irl.coop is a peer group that exists alongside emergent groups, not above them: it stewards the technology stack and redistributes funds to groups that exhibit cooperative metrics and match the priorities its own governance lays out (loosely starting from the verticals below). A group's connection to irl.coop is always by choice, never by default:

  • irl.coop — a steward, not a root. A group like any other (a Safe), whose members are the people and groups who have joined it. It stewards the stack and holds the funds it redistributes. It is nobody's automatic parent.
  • Vertical funds (subgroup-of irl.coop). irl.coop's own funding pools, one per priority. A group receives sponsored-by funding from a vertical when it matches that priority and demonstrates cooperative metrics — an opt-in relationship, not a nesting.
  • Autonomous groups (peer to irl.coop). Anyone who signs up to use the platform gets their own group, not a seat in irl.coop. Groups form and operate independently of irl.coop; the only default relationship is that they use its tools.

Joining the coop. Membership in irl.coop is opt-in, and it is what grants a voice. When a group covers its cost (the pay-what-you-want model), it is strongly encouraged to join the coop — to become a member of the irl.coop Safe — so it has a say in how the coop uses funds and how the platform works. Funding and membership are separate edges: a group can be sponsored-by a vertical without being a member, and can be a member without receiving funding.

Multi-valued axes. A group is not confined to one vertical: "food sovereignty" and "ecological repair" both describe the growing team, so it holds a sponsored-by edge to each. The edges are declared, not inherited — nothing about the vertical is implied beyond the funding terms on the edge.

Cross-cutting verticals. Education is a vertical of its own, but its product — the zk certificate — is consumed across the others: food-safety for Food Skills, kayaker certs for the Meetup, first-aid for Childcare. That is the proofs axis of a shape doing its job: one vertical issues a credential, another vertical's guard conditions consume it.

The vertical names here are illustrative. The design docs name the actual sub-verticals as Land Back, Solidarity Economies, Ecological Repair, Community Safety, Healing Justice, Cultural Power, and Relational Infrastructure — the diagram below arranges one candidate subset, plus the cross-cutting Education vertical, to show the meta-group pattern rather than to fix the taxonomy.

A.5  The Tier-3 zk certificate, in practice

A Tier-3 certificate is a portable credential: it lets an individual prove "I'm qualified" without proving "who I am," and lets a group prove "we ran a covered operation" without revealing its roster. The badge is an off-chain commitment — commit(learner_sub, course_id, outcome, issued_at, expiry, certifier_quorum) — issued by a quorum (no single assessor may mint one), bound to the Tier-2 assessment chain, and fresh (the proof binds a validity window). The outcome is a range proof: "assessed ≥ passing" is proven, the exact grade is not — a 98 and a 71 both prove "passed" identically.

Individual → group. A stranger filling a safety-kayaker slot on a public swim runs the prover on their own device (the witness never leaves it) and submits a proof asserting "I hold cert K from Education group E, currently valid." The meetup's gate verifies against E's public commitment root — learning that a qualified person is in the slot, and nothing else: no name, no grade, no other certificates, no sub. On success the swimmer is granted a scoped, expiring kayaker role for that RSVP window only. Anti-Sybil reuses the treasury's nullifier: each presentation includes nullifier = H(cert_secret, event_id), so one cert fills at most one slot per event while remaining unlinkable across events.

Group → authority. Renewing its open-water permit, the meetup produces one aggregate proof over the season's Tier-2 records: "every swim met the 1:3 coverage ratio, and the kayaker on duty held a valid cert at the time." The authority verifies and accepts — the group reveals the property (safe coverage), never the records (who, when, how many). If a deeper audit is needed, a project viewing key opens only the coverage-ledger slice. This is "compliance on demand, not by default."

Honest limit: a ZK proof vouches for the claim, never the truth of the underlying data — a captured assessor quorum mints worthless badges no matter how crisp the cryptography. That is why certification is quorum-gated and the bad-actor signal is reputation-derived but human-gated. The cryptography hides identity; it cannot fix governance.

A.6  Four institutional scenarios: formation → federation

Three further scenarios show the model's arc: each begins as a single group and, when it scales, becomes a meta-group by the same edges — subgroup-of for shared identity and sponsored-by for funding. They also exercise the model's least-obvious machinery: the hidden tier, the guards, and the coordination family.

⚒️ Worker union — in formation, then after formation

In formation (hidden). Workers at a non-union workplace organize before a card-signing drive. This is the model's hidden tier made load-bearing: privacy = hidden, provable-but-not-enumerable membership. Each worker holds a ZK membership proof — "I'm with the union" — presentable to a fellow organizer without the employer ever seeing a roster. Grievances are collected as anonymized stories (the solutions-library primitive); the organizer does outreach through the coordination family (a rep handoff, a first-come volunteer queue). No dues yet, no treasury — just a Safe, seats, a hidden Matrix room, and the membership commitment tree.

After formation (recognized). Recognition flips privacy to members — the bargaining unit is now disclosed, because the employer must know who is covered. Dues are Tier-1 inflows; the strike fund is a reserve — 3-of-5 signatures, a timelock on outflow, and a circuit-breaker freeze (the guards of §11). Contract ratification is governance: members sign the contract hash with EIP-1271, quorum = % of the owner set. Strike pay is a distribution fund, auto-split per the entitlement table. A local may subgroup-of a national labor federation — an emergent meta-group, like the block: the federation shares its site and name, the local's Safe stays its own. Funding from irl.coop is separate and opt-in: the local receives sponsored-by support from the Solidarity Economies vertical only when it matches the priority and demonstrates cooperative metrics.

⛪ Church — a congregation, then a network

A single congregation. Roles pastor · elder · deacon · member · visitor (the visitor is an external guest). Tithes are the funding pool; the benevolence fund is a distribution account with discreet giving — the deacon sees a coverage proof ("we helped N households this month"), never each giver's amount (project viewing keys). Services are events & RSVP; pastoral care is the coordination family (a deacon reaches out = a handoff); small groups are Matrix rooms; the sermon archive is MinIO storage.

A group of churches. Congregations subgroup-of a shared body (the denomination or a local association): one website across the network (the A.3 pattern), a shared benevolence pool whose Safe is the body's fund, but each congregation keeps its own treasury, its own seats, and its own budget. Cross-denominational federation stays parked — the edges are declared, not inherited.

🏫 Student club — one campus, then many

One campus chapter. Roles president · treasurer · officer · member · guest. Dues + university funding form the pool; the budget is an operating fund — 2-of-3 signatures and per-ledger category limits (the guards). Meetings and workshops are events & RSVP; volunteer hours are Tier-2 labor credit; the "how we pulled this off" write-ups are the solutions library. The treasurer proves "we are within budget" to the student government via a coverage proof without exposing line items.

Across many campuses. A national organization is the meta-group; each campus chapter subgroup-of it — a shared national site, shared dues remittance into the national Safe, but each chapter's Safe settles its own events. Cross-campus, a member presents a ZK membership proof ("I'm a current member of chapter Y") to attend another campus's event without the host learning their identity, or a zk-badge ("current student at X") for eligibility. The multi-campus org is the A.3 "one website, many groups" pattern scaled from a block to a continent.

🗳️ Civic engagement — a chapter, then a network

A chapter. A local civic-engagement group runs the whole catalogue of civic work. Voter-registration drives are events & RSVP with voters as external guests. Poll-worker recruitment is the matcher — recruits are paired to precincts, and election-day coverage is a rotation/coverage constraint (every precinct needs N workers; poll-worker training is a zk-badge). Ride-sharing to the polls runs the matcher again (rides ↔ riders), and escorts in hostile territories are the coordination family — with the hidden tier made load-bearing: a rider or escort proves "I'm a vetted volunteer" without the roster ever being enumerable, and the ride itself is private. Know-your-rights trainings are the Education shape — cohorts, labor credit, a zk-badge, and anonymous attendance. Advocacy days in state houses and town halls are events & RSVP with coordination routing who testifies. Calling lists and postcards are the labor-credit economy (calls and cards as Tier-2 credits); protests are events with marshals on a coverage rota.

A network. Chapters subgroup-of a state or national coalition (the Civic Engagement vertical) — one site, shared call-lists and ride infrastructure, per-chapter treasuries. The coalition's Safe holds the shared funds (postcards, printing, buses); each chapter keeps its own seats, and a chapter operating in a hostile territory stays privacy = hidden even inside the coalition — the parent sees that it exists, never its roster.

A.7  Peer production, contests, and curated lists

Five more shapes. Three form a family — research, study, and writing groups share one skeleton — and two exercise distinctive machinery: the writing contest (anonymized judging + a prize pool) and the dance list (group curation + a federated listing).

The peer-production family

Research working groups, study groups, and writing groups differ mainly in output and formality, not structure. All three run on the same four primitives — a calendar (deadlines, cohorts, workshops), labor credit (research hours, teaching, feedback), the matcher (collaborators, study partners, beta readers), and anonymized review (drafts are critiqued before attribution is revealed, so a senior author and a newcomer get the same read). They are the Education shape (A.2) minus the certifier role: teaching and learning without a credential. In the meta-group they slot under Education (study group, research working group) and Cultural Power (writing group).

🏆 Writing contest — the NYC Midnight pattern

A contest accepting submissions for judging by a rotating panel, with anonymized entries, a prize pool, and publication of the winners. Its flow is a loop of rounds: a prompt (genre, word limit, deadline) → entrants submit under a pen name → a rotating panel reads and scores the anonymized entries → placement (winner, shortlist, honorable mention) → the winner's identity is revealed at the award → winners publish in an anthology, magazine, compilation, or on the web. Five mechanisms carry it:

  • Anonymized judging. "Anonymization is owned by the source" (§10): the contest system holds the author→pen-name map, and the judging view carries only the pen name and the text. Judges never see the author — the same principle as the solutions library's anonymized stories.
  • A rotating panel. Rotation/coverage (§10) assigns which judges read which round, so no single judge reads every entry and no pair of judges colludes across rounds. Judges are external guests — scoped seats with read-and-score rights and no governance stake.
  • Conflict-of-interest exclusion. A judge "too close" to an entrant in the relationship graph is auto-excluded from that entry's panel — the §12 bounded-distance proof (dist(judge, entrant) ≥ K, K ∈ {1,2,3}), so closeness is excluded without revealing who is close to whom.
  • The prize pool. The group's Safe is the pool (there is no separate prize account); the winner is paid via the distribution fund per the entitlement table — a single distribution for the winner, a split for shortlist and honorable mention. A vertical fund can sponsored-by the contest to seed the pool.
  • Publication. The anthology is collective selling (book, magazine, or compilation) plus Webstudio (web) and MinIO (print-ready files). Entry is opt-in; the prize is not contingent on publication.

The privacy-hardened variant is a commitment: an entry is submitted as commit(entry, author, round), judged on the text (the commitment is opened for the text, the author stays hidden), and only the winner's commitment is opened at the award — "provable but not enumerable" (§12) applied to authorship. Version 0 is the pen-name form above; the commitment form is the deferred, harder path, and only matters if a contest wants judges to be provably unable to unmask an entrant.

💃 Dance list — curated, and federated

A group-curated list of upcoming dance events in a locale, federated so people from multiple regions see every region's events on one website. Roles: curator, contributor, dancer, and (external) event organizer. Two mechanisms carry it:

  • Curation. The list is a projection, not a store: events live as events & RSVP, and the list renders a curated subset. Two signals, as in the blueprint library (§9): curator-picked (moderated) and community-signaled (highlighted = attendance + upvote count). Curators hold a scoped seat, not ownership — the event organizer keeps their own event data.
  • One website, many regions. Each locale is a child group subgroup-of a parent "dance" group. Each child curates its region's events and keeps its own seats, treasury, and curation authority; the parent site unions every locale's list. This is the A.3 pattern — a shared namespace and site, not full federation. True cross-domain federation (separate domains, takedown-resilient DNS) is parked; the shared-site form already delivers "see every region's in-person events on one website."

The matcher surfaces events near a dancer or on their travel dates; the calendar renders the union across locales. It works for dance specifically because events are local (a venue, a date) but discovery is global — the subgroup-of pattern gives global discovery with local curation authority. It slots under Cultural Power in the meta-group.

A.8  The weave

The weave is how people — or an advanced, RAG-fueled, scoped AI inference engine — coordinate to meet a complex need. It is not the machinery; it is the intelligence that drives it. Five steps:

  1. Sense — detect or sense complex needs, possibly held by multiple parties.
  2. Synthesize & connect — understand and synthesize the solution space (sometimes negotiating), and connect the needs to offers, possibly across multiple parties.
  3. Fill gaps — determine what is missing, and fill it with a new "glue" need — or fulfill it directly.
  4. Secure & complete — establish communication, protection for the exchange, contingencies, and the collective understanding of what "complete" feels like.
  5. Steward — optionally steward the creation of a group from this configuration, to perpetuate the arrangement as a self-sustaining, managed entity.

The pathways meet the weave at specific steps: the matcher is step 2; the guards and escrow are step 4; provisioning a group from a shape is step 5; the solutions library — anonymized stories of how a need was met — is the memory that feeds step 1 and step 2.

Threaded example — "manure for hay," done properly as a weave: someone senses that the fields need fertilizing and the stable needs clearing; the weave synthesizes "manure for hay" and connects Jesse (surplus hay) to Priya (need for manure); it fills the gap — nobody to haul it — with a new glue need ("a truck and a driver"), which Jesse happens to fill; it establishes protection (both confirm before it counts as done) and what "complete" feels like (hay delivered, manure spread); and, if the swap recurs each season, it may steward a standing arrangement — a group — to perpetuate it.
